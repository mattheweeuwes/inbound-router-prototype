# Decisions

One short entry each. Where the code departs from a plain reading of the original brief, the entry says so.

## 1. Key accounts on stable public IDs, never names

Accounts are `ahrq:<health_sys_id>`, `ccn:<ccn>`, `payer:<slug>`, `npi:<npi>`, or `domain:<domain>` as a last resort; a group from the rate file is `ein:<EIN>` (decision 20). A hospital that belongs to an AHRQ system resolves to the system and keeps its CCN in `ids_json`. Names are only used to find a candidate, never as the key, so a rename or a typo does not split an account. When the name matches several accounts equally, the lead goes to a person with the top five candidates (rule R6) instead of being guessed.

## 2. Resolve before you score

Fit points depend on who the organization is, so scoring an unplaced lead produces a confident number about nothing. Resolution runs first, in a fixed order that stops at the first confident answer: curated domain, known organization domain, company-name match, homepage title, NPPES, TLD heuristic. The known-domain step runs before the TLD step on purpose, so `jhmi.edu` is Johns Hopkins Health System and not "academic". Every step is written to `resolution.evidence`. A lead that cannot be placed gets a human, not a silent guess. The one inference is a benefits or HR contact at an unknown company, who is resolved as a probable self-funded employer, labeled low confidence and routed by tier (decision 12). An account already in the CRM mirror that the public registries do not know keeps the CRM record's identity, segment and size (method `crm`); a registry match is never overridden.

## 3. The model classifies; code writes facts

The model returns an enum record (use case, urgency, buying role, disqualifier, confidence) with its reasons first. It never writes text a prospect sees. The model cannot set urgency, override a rule, write alert text or get a lead dropped on its word alone. Its use-case label can change the team for API-type leads (`teamFor` in `src/route.ts`). The rep brief in Slack and the CRM task is extractive: the prospect's own words in quotes, plus facts inserted by code from the registry (hospital count, beds, match method). Nothing in an alert is generated.

## 4. Disqualify on rules; a model alone cannot drop a lead

A rule hit (disposable domain, student title, job-seeker language, spam wording, and so on) disqualifies. When only the model says a lead is a non-buyer, spam and vendor pitch always go to a person: those two are dropped without anyone reading them, so a model-only call at any confidence, 0.8 or higher included, clears the disqualifier and sends the lead to review (R6) with reason `llm_only_disqualifier`. For the other disqualifiers (job seeker, student, media) the model alone counts at confidence 0.8 or above, recorded as the model's call; below that the same review path applies. The model never decides "competitor"; that comes from the curated domain list. A buyer is never dropped on the model's word alone. When the model runs after the alert (decision 19), the same model-only calls arrive as a doubt: a review item with reason `model_doubt_after_alert` and a follow-up, not a drop or a silent change of route.

## 5. Account owner beats round-robin

When the resolved account is in the CRM mirror (customer, open opportunity, partner), the owner gets the lead whatever the tier (R5). The tier still sets the SLA. This is what keeps two reps from working one account. Round-robin (least recently assigned, skipping anyone at capacity) only applies to accounts nobody owns. An account becomes owned the first time a round-robin rep alert names a rep (tier A under R8, or an enterprise deal under R10, decision 25): that rep is written to `accounts.owner` and the claim to `account_claims` in the same transaction as the assignment (standing in for HubSpot's company owner being set by the company upsert, whose body then carries `hubspot_owner_id`), and the next lead from that account goes to the same rep under R5. A seeded CRM-mirror owner always wins over a claim. Team-queue, nurture, review and web-intent routes claim nothing, and resetting the demo clears claims with the rest of the database.

## 6. Idempotent webhooks

`(source, source_event_id)` is unique; a re-delivery answers 200 `duplicate: true` and does nothing else. The id comes from the payload's own timestamp, or from a SHA-256 of the canonical JSON body when there is none; server time never enters it. The event and its lead are inserted in one transaction. Separately, the same normalized email within 30 days attaches as a new touch (`duplicate_of`, same owner, repeat-engagement points), but only to an earlier lead that went to sales (a rep alert, owner task, team queue or review). A guide download that went to nurture counts as a prior touch for scoring and does not swallow the demo request that follows it: that request is routed normally, with its own rep and CRM task. Re-running a lead replaces its derived rows and the CRM writes upsert, but a re-run (boot recovery) can repeat an alert. "Earlier" means accepted earlier, within 30 days either side of the lead's own time, not an earlier source timestamp (decision 24). A repeat touch does not alert inside 24 hours of the last alert (a list digest counts as one); after that, only a tier-A touch alerts again. The first rule protects against the sender, the second against the prospect.

## 7. 202 and asynchronous processing

The inbound handler verifies, records and answers 202; the pipeline runs after, in this order: normalize, resolve, qualify, score, route, alert, then the HubSpot write. Here that is an in-process queue that prepares concurrently and commits in order (decision 19): each lead's order-independent work starts at once, four at a time, and an ordered section then takes one lead at a time for everything that depends on earlier leads. Unfinished leads (including those waiting for a retry, see decision 16) are picked up again at boot, in the order they were accepted (`lead_events.accepted_at`, the server's clock at the 202; `received_at` stays the payload's own time). The HubSpot delivery is not part of that queue (decision 15). In production it should be a durable queue (Vercel Queues, Inngest or SQS) with a dead-letter queue and parallel workers. `time_to_route_ms` is measured from receipt (the 202) to the routing decision, because that is what the prospect experiences. The part of it spent waiting for earlier leads in the ordered section is also reported as `timings.queued`, so a burst shows up as queue time rather than as a slow pipeline; the part spent waiting for the lead's own prepared work is `timings.waitedForPrepare`. The demo replay defaults to 2000 ms between posts.

## 8. Dry-run CRM writes, company upsert on a unique property

HubSpot and Slack payloads are built exactly and stored on the lead and in `out/`, and by default nothing is sent. The Slack alert is built and recorded before the HubSpot write, so a slow or failing CRM never delays the rep: nothing in the alert comes from the CRM stage. Live delivery goes through an outbox, with retries and a dead state (decision 15). Live HubSpot needs a service key and `HUBSPOT_LIVE=1`; it has run that way against a developer test portal. Companies are upserted on `serif_account_key`, a unique-value custom property holding the stable account key, so a company is the same company whether it arrived as `intermountainhealthcare.org`, a Gmail address with a company name, or an event badge. Keying on domain or name would create duplicates. Two endpoint shapes were missing from the documentation; the test-portal run confirmed both (see `DATA-CONTRACT.md`).

## 9. A model slot, rules first, with an honest eval

This demo fills the model slot with `llama3.2:3b` on Ollama at temperature 0, constrained to a JSON schema. Rules run on every lead. The provider sits behind one interface, so a hosted model can replace it and be run against the same eval sets (`npm run eval`). At Serif the slot is filled by evidence, not preference: candidates such as a larger open-weights model (Qwen or GLM class) on Serif's hardware, or a hosted model under Serif's data terms (the Anthropic provider in `src/qualify/llm.ts`, off unless keyed), run against a labelled set of Serif's own past leads, and one replaces the baseline only by beating it at the same floors. Use case, the weakest field on the sealed set, is where a stronger model would earn its cost first. The rules guard, and a person on an unsure "no", stay whatever fills the slot. The model is only called for leads that include something a person wrote; guide, badge and usage-only leads go to rules alone. Of those, only a lead whose use case the rules cannot settle waits for the model before it is routed; the rest are routed on the rules and get the same call after the alert (decision 19).

Combine policy v4 (`COMBINE_VERSION`, in `src/qualify/combine.ts`) is what the pipeline runs. v1, v2 and v3 stay callable for the comparison.

- v1: a confident model overrides the rules on use case, urgency and role.
- v2: rules first, the model fills gaps; urgency from a short list of keyword cues.
- v3: v2, with urgency decided by a temporal-expression parser (`src/qualify/when.ts`); the model is asked when the parser finds nothing.
- v4: v3, but the model never decides urgency.

v4 per field:
- Urgency: the parser reads the message, resolves every date expression to a concrete date relative to when the lead was received (America/New_York), and takes the earliest future one. "ASAP" or an equivalent is now; a horizon of 92 days or less is now, up to 183 days is this quarter, beyond that is exploring. Budget or planning language with no nearer date is this quarter. Exploring or no-rush language counts only when no date is present; an explicit date always beats it. "No rush" and "not urgent" can never read as urgent, and "reply now" style imperatives are not deadlines. If the message has no timing language, urgency is `unknown`. The one exception is a lead with no free text at all, which keeps the door default (exploring for product doors). The cue, the resolved date and the day count are stored in `qualification.rules.evidence` (`urgencyCue`, `urgencyDate`, `urgencyDays`, `urgencyKind`), and the Slack "why now" line quotes them (for example `("mid-January", 2027-01-15, 70 days out)`). Nothing in that line comes from the model.
- Use case: a clear keyword winner stands (2 or more points and half a point ahead, or 1 point and a full point ahead); otherwise the model if valid and confident; else the rules' answer.
- Buying role: from the title when it carries a seniority, else the model.
- Disqualifier: a rule hit disqualifies. For spam and vendor pitch, a model-only call goes to a person at any confidence. For the other disqualifiers the model alone counts at 0.8 or above, and below that the lead goes to review.

Why the model does not decide urgency: the small demo model got dates wrong (it called "January" six months out), and when a message gives no timing it tends to volunteer one anyway (`exploring` or `this_quarter`), where the honest answer is that the prospect gave none. A timeline the prospect never stated adds points to the intent score without evidence, so a lead can reach a rep alert on a guess. In v3 that showed: with the model consulted for the silent messages, held-out urgency was 17/24, and 22/24 without it. This was a product decision made before any sealed data existed, not a result of tuning. A larger model would make fewer of these errors, and the parser would still decide: date math and the urgency cut-offs are rules, and code applies them the same way every time, at no cost.

Every field records which side decided it (`qualification.final.sources`: `rules` for a keyword or parser decision, `model`, or `default` for the door default or unknown) and whether the model and the rules agreed (`agreement`).

## 10. What changes with Serif's real stack

- HubSpot forms and workflows replace the fixture forms; the same payload adapter sits behind a form webhook.
- Calendly webhook signing is implemented against the documented header format; the signing key comes from the Calendly subscription.
- The Clay web-intent webhook replaces the `web_intent` fixture. It is account-level, so it feeds account intent and a research task, never a rep alert.
- PostHog events, or warehouse rows, feed product-qualified-lead scoring in place of the three usage rules.
- Databricks plus Hightouch (or similar) reverse ETL of account scores: `npm run reverse-etl` builds the company batch upsert from the `v_account_scores` view, which is the shape a warehouse model would hand to the sync.
- Closed-won and closed-lost outcomes should feed back into the weights in `src/score.ts`. Today they are hand-set.

## 11. Provider groups with no single NPI

NPPES is searched for an unplaced lead with a provider-looking company. One organizational NPI-2 match becomes `npi:<npi>`. A brand that spans several NPI-2 records (for example a practice platform with one record per state) has no single NPI, so it is keyed on `domain:<domain>` with the NPIs listed in `firmographics.npis`, and the evidence says so. NPPES matches registered other names as well as legal names.

## 12. Employer inference is labeled

An unplaced corporate domain with a benefits or HR title (or a network-and-benefits use case) is set to `employer_self_funded` with `confidence: low` and `inferred: true`. It scores as an unknown-size employer (no size points). It is not presented as a registry match.

## 13. Signed doors fail closed; the eval is a gate with a freeze record

A door that is meant to be signed (today only Calendly, listed in `SIGNED_DOORS` in `src/sources/index.ts`) refuses every request with 503 when its key is missing or blank. Skipping the check when no key is set would let an unsigned request create a lead, so an unconfigured door is closed, not open. The server logs one warning line at start naming each signed door without a key. Every default lives in code (`makeConfig()` in `src/config.ts`); `.env.example` is documentation only and is never loaded, because loading it once handed the door a key and the 503 path could never fire. `npm run dev` and `npm run replay` set the local demo key inline, and `npm run dev` also sets `DEMO_ROUTES=1`.

`npm run eval:gate` runs the normal evaluation and exits 1 if combine v4 is below the floors on the sealed set (urgency 22/24, use case 17/24, disqualifier 21/24) or the held-out set (21/24, 19/24, 22/24), each one regression below the Nov 7 results (on the Nov 9 re-run the sealed disqualifier result is exactly at its floor). If the local model cannot be reached it fails; it never passes by skipping. The floors are set in `scripts/lib/eval-gate.ts` and no classifier code or label is changed to meet them.

`out/eval-manifest.json` records the SHA-256 of the sealed set and of every classifier source file (everything under `src/qualify` plus the runtime imports of those files), one combined classifier hash, the model, temperature and prompt, the timestamp and the per-set results. The next run compares the classifier hash with the recorded one. This makes any later edit to the sealed set or the classifier detectable, as the Nov 9 run showed: the manifest flagged the classifier as changed against the Nov 7 record. The current record is `docs/eval-record-2026-11-09.md` with `docs/eval-manifest-2026-11-09.json`; the Nov 7 pair is history. It cannot show that the sealed set was written before the parser existed; that still rests on the set's own note and on file modification times, which the manifest records as supporting evidence only.

## 14. A local server stays local, and the one outbound fetch is checked

The server binds to 127.0.0.1 unless `HOST` says otherwise. The cockpit's write routes (`/api/demo/submit`, `/api/demo/replay`) answer 404 unless `DEMO_ROUTES=1`; the read endpoints are unauthenticated, which is acceptable only because of the bind address.

The only fetch aimed at a name a stranger supplied is the homepage title of an unknown email domain. The domain must be a plain DNS hostname (no port, path, query or IP literal), the name is resolved first and refused if any answer is a private, loopback, link-local, CGNAT, unique-local or otherwise reserved address (IPv4 and IPv6), and redirects must stay on https and DNS names, at most 3. A failed fetch is cached for an hour and the cache holds at most 5,000 domains. One gap remains: the name is resolved once to check it and again by `fetch`, so a DNS answer that changes in between (rebinding) is not caught. Closing it needs the connection pinned to the checked address, which the platform `fetch` does not allow without a custom dispatcher.

## 15. HubSpot writes go through an outbox

The CRM stage plans the requests and stores them; it never calls HubSpot. In dry-run it writes the plan to `out/hubspot/` and `crm_json`, as before, and is finished. In live mode it stores the plan in `crm_outbox` and in `crm_json` (`delivery: pending`), and the lead is `done`: its alert is out and its plan cannot be lost. A worker (`src/crm/outbox.ts`) delivers plans one at a time, oldest first, outside the pipeline's one-at-a-time section. Before this, the HubSpot call ran inside that section with a 10 s timeout and one retry, so an unreachable HubSpot held every lead behind it for up to 20 s; the alert already went out first, but the next lead's did not.

A call that throws, or a 429 or 5xx answer, stops the plan at that request and is retried with backoff: 2 s doubling to a 5 minute cap, or the `Retry-After` the answer asked for when that is longer (capped at an hour), on an in-process timer, up to 8 attempts (about four minutes). Then the plan is `dead`: `crm_json` says so with the last error, the dashboard counts its requests as failed and the HubSpot line turns red, and the attempt is in the activity feed. Any other 4xx is recorded and not retried, because the same request would get the same answer. Re-sending a whole plan is safe for the reasons it was before: contacts upsert on email, companies on `serif_account_key`, the association PUT is a no-op when it exists, and a task is created only when no open task with the same subject is there (with HubSpot's search lag as the one known gap).

Delivery is strictly in order across all leads: while the oldest plan waits for its retry, the ones behind it wait too. Per-contact ordering would let unrelated leads through, but HubSpot outages and rate limits are portal-wide, and global order is the simplest rule that never lets a later write for a contact land before an earlier one. A poisoned plan holds the queue for its four minutes, then goes dead. `crm_json` keeps the fields everything reads (`mode`, `requests`, `skipped`, `ok`, `responses`, `ownerMapped`), filled when delivery settles, plus `delivery`, `attempts`, `error` and `nextAttemptAt`. A re-run drops its lead's plan if it is still pending, and a delivery that finishes after that does not overwrite the new record. In production this is the same shape on a durable queue with a dead-letter queue.

## 16. A failed run is retried, then handed to a person

Before, a lead whose pipeline threw was set to `failed` and left: no retry, nobody told, and boot recovery skipped it. Now the run is retried with backoff (5 s, then 10 s) while the lead has attempts left; it waits as status `retrying`, which the dashboard counts as in flight and boot recovery picks up. After the third failure the lead is `failed`, the RevOps alert payload is recorded through the same Slack recorder as every other alert (`#revops-review`, with the door, the received time, the attempt count and the last error) and stored on the lead as `alert_json.failure`, and the dashboard's failed count is the number of leads given up. Every failed run leaves a `pipeline` row in `stage_log` that a later run does not wipe, so the history stays. A run that failed after its alert went out can send that alert again when it is retried; that is the same trade boot recovery already made.

## 17. Account claims in their own table

`account_claims` (lead id, account, owner, time) is what a claim is. A re-run releases its lead's claims by lead id, and only while the account is still held by the rep that lead put there. Before, the re-run found the claim by reading the rep's name back out of the human-readable signal text, taking the first word after the id, so a rep named "Avery Smith" was looked up as "Avery", the claim was never released, and the re-run routed the lead to its own claim under R5. The signal row stays for the cockpit, which shows it under the account; signals now carry `lead_id` in a column instead of a `[id]` prefix in the text, and nothing parses the text.

## 18. Migrations run once

`openDb` used to run every file in `sql/migrations` on every boot, which only worked while every statement was `CREATE ... IF NOT EXISTS`. The runner in `src/db.ts` applies each file once, in name order, each in its own transaction, and records it in `schema_migrations`, so a later file can `ALTER TABLE` safely and a failed file leaves nothing half-applied. A database from before the runner has 001 applied but not recorded; 001 is all `IF NOT EXISTS`, so it runs again as a no-op and is recorded, and 002 carries the old rows into the new shape: claims from the signal rows (the owner from `accounts.owner`, which a claim always set), the `[id]` prefix moved into `account_signals.lead_id`, writes marked `retryPending` into the outbox, due now, and `accepted_at` from `leads.created_at`. `test/migrate.test.ts` builds such a database from the old schema and checks that it opens, migrates and renders.

## 19. The model leaves the path to the alert, except where it can change the route

Before, the model ran before routing for every lead with free text, inside the one-lead-at-a-time section. Under combine v4 it can change only three things: the use case when the rules have no clear keyword winner (`rulesUseCaseIsClear` false), the buying role when the title carries no seniority, and a disqualifier the rules missed. It never sets urgency, and the buying role carries no points and no routing. On the 40-event demo day it was called on 28 of 39 leads and changed a final field on one (a use case); the other 27 calls changed nothing the route read and sat in front of every rep alert. (It was also the source of record for one buying role, where it said `unknown`, the same as the rules.)

Now (`modelTiming` in `src/decisions.ts`, `src/modelPath.ts`):
- A lead with free text whose use case the rules cannot settle waits for the model, because the use case can change the team. That lead is the only kind that holds its alert for the model.
- Every other free-text lead is routed and alerted on the rules, and the same model call runs after the alert as a second opinion (`src/secondOpinion.ts`), through the same combine and guard rails. It stores what the model said, whether the full combine agrees with the route, which fields it would have changed and the buying role it fills (`leads.second_opinion_json`). The route record (`qualification_json`) keeps what the route used.
- If the second opinion raises a disqualifier no rule raised, the lead is not quietly un-routed. On a route the old order would have changed or annotated (R5 or R8) it opens a review item with reason `model_doubt_after_alert` and records a follow-up to where the alert went and to `#revops-review`, worded like the R5 and R6 reasons: what the model flagged, that no rule agreed, why that is not acted on alone, that RevOps is checking it and that the owner keeps the lead unless RevOps withdraws it. Nothing the model wrote goes into it. On a route an earlier rule decided (a drop, a repeat touch, a competitor, R4, or R6 itself) the doubt is recorded only, because the old order would have routed it the same way.
- Second opinions run one at a time, oldest first, in a model slot that a route's call always goes ahead of, and only while no lead is waiting in the ordered section, because a model call cannot be interrupted and the next lead may need the model. The queue is the leads table (`second_opinion = 'pending'`), so a stopped process loses nothing: boot asks again, including a call that was in flight.
- `MODEL_BEFORE_ROUTE=all` restores the old placement: the model before the route for every free-text lead, and no second opinions. Rules-only mode is unchanged (the rules provider answers at once, so its leads keep the record they always had).

The trade-off: before, a doubt only the model had held the lead for RevOps before any rep was alerted. Now the rep hears in milliseconds and a doubt, when there is one, follows within seconds (one model call, about 2.5 s, plus any wait for a lull in the queue). So a rep can occasionally see a lead that RevOps then withdraws. Leads where the model can change the team still wait for it. On the demo day no second opinion raised a doubt, so no lead was routed differently; across the labelled sets (the dev day, held-out and sealed) the gated path routes no item on different classifier inputs than the full combine (`npm run eval`, Fast path section).

Prepare concurrently, commit in order (`src/pipeline.ts`, `src/stages.ts`). Each lead's order-independent work (adapt, normalize, resolve with its network lookups, the rules and the gated model call) starts when the lead is accepted, at most `PREPARE_CONCURRENCY` (4) at once, and writes nothing to the database. The ordered section then takes the leads one at a time, in the order they were accepted, waits for that lead's prepared result, and does what depends on earlier leads: the prior-touch and duplicate lookup, the account upsert, score, route, round-robin and claim, alert and the CRM plan. Round-robin and dedupe stay race-free, and leads commit in the order they were accepted. One lead that waits for the model still holds the leads accepted after it, because they commit in order. Decision 23 lets leads in other lanes pass it.

`time_to_route_ms` keeps its definition (the 202 to the routing decision). `timings_json` adds `prepared`, `waitedForPrepare`, `prepare` and `modelBeforeRoute`, and the cockpit's speed card shows time to alert, the model call routes waited for, and the second opinions after the alert below a divider, off the path.

## 20. Outbound from the rate file: the EIN is the key, links are never guessed, figures come from code

The take-home's outbound analysis (Part 1) runs as a module (`src/outbound/`), loaded from Serif's public rate file at startup when the file's hash, the platform mapping, the registry or the derivation (`OUTBOUND_VERSION`) changes. Four rules carry over from the router.

- **The EIN is the key.** A group in the rate file is keyed `ein:<EIN>`, the file's own stable id for the contracting entity, never its name: the same entity can appear under several spellings across files and months. Its company upsert uses the same unique property as every other company (`serif_account_key`).
- **A link is never guessed.** Each group is also linked to a registry organization by name and state, through the same matcher and thresholds the router uses for a company field (`resolveByName` in `src/resolve/index.ts`, over `matchCompany`). Only a unique, strong match links. A tie or a weak match is stored unlinked, with its candidates, the score of the best one and the reason, which is how a lead with the same doubt goes to a person (R6). There are no network lookups in this step. On the August 2026 file, 12 of the 127 groups link; a group whose link is a hospital or health system is not a target.
- **A draft is blocked until its platform mapping is confirmed.** The file names entities, not their parents, so which EIN belongs to which behavioral health platform is a hand-made mapping (`fixtures/outbound-platforms.json`), each entry with a `confirmed` flag. A confirmed entry gives a target with a draft marked ready for a rep. An unconfirmed one (the Oklahoma City EIN named PsychPlus, for one) gives a target whose draft is marked blocked with the reason, because an entity is only called a platform's when it is. Small independent groups are the subject of the finding, not its buyer, and hospital systems hold the leverage it describes, so neither is a target; any group can still be rendered as a labelled preview.
- **Code inserts every figure.** The email (a subject and five sentences) and the LinkedIn follow-up are fixed templates with no digits in them. Each placeholder is filled from a named field (the finding, the file's terms, the patterns, or a constant named as such), and the draft returns every inserted value with its field; a test checks that every number in the rendered text is one of them. No model writes any part of it. `{first_name}` stays a placeholder: there are no contact people. Each EIN always gets the same one of the take-home's two hooks (hospital leverage, or the rate range small groups sit in), half and half by a hash of the EIN, so the two can be compared on replies.

The patterns are found by search, as the take-home found them: every distinct rate from $100 to $200 is tried as the card's base (a rate counts when it is a whole percent of it, 50% to 300%, to the cent), and every amount from $2.00 to $10.00 is tried as the unit (a rate counts when it is an exact whole multiple). A pattern needs at least three distinct rates, because every base explains itself. The take-home's chance check (the same search on randomly nudged rates) is random and not ported; the hit counts are stored for a reader to judge. On the August 2026 file the module reproduces the take-home's published numbers exactly (`test/outbound-parity.test.ts`, run when the file is present), and on Nov 9 it matched every field of the take-home's 127 merge-field rows (group, city, state, clinicians, rate, tier, factor, percent of Medicare, city top rate and its size).

Nothing is sent. The company upsert carrying the finding is built for target groups with the router's request builder and shown by the API; it is never put in the outbox, even with `HUBSPOT_LIVE=1`, because outbound writes to a CRM want a person's approval first. There is no email code and no sequence call.

The loop back into the router: when an inbound lead's organization is linked to a rate group, or a group has its normalized name in its state, the brief, the Slack alert and the CRM task carry one line of fact about that group's rate (`Their UHC Choice Plus rate for 90837 (Aug 2026): $163.09, 97% of Medicare, a $47 conversion factor; top rate in Dallas $378.38.`), stored on the lead as `rate_fact_json`. It is read in the notify stage, after the route, and nothing that scores or routes reads it; a test checks that a lead's score and route are the same with and without a rate file, and the CI replay's decisions are unchanged from the commit before the module.

## 22. Product usage is tied to pipeline as influence, on invented accounts

Serif has PostHog for product analytics and HubSpot for the CRM, and nobody has joined them, so feature work cannot be tied to sales. The usage module (`src/usage/`, `sql/models/v_usage_*.sql`) joins them on the account key the router already uses.

- **Influence, not weighted multi-touch.** A feature influenced a deal when its account used it in the 90 days before the deal was created or while it was open. That is one definition a sales leader can check against a single deal (the cockpit lists the deals behind each feature), and it fits in one SQL view. A weighted multi-touch model needs credit rules (linear, time-decay, position-based) that nobody can validate at this volume: the fixtures have 22 deals, and weights fitted on that few would be invented precision with a decimal point on it. So a deal counts fully under every feature it was around, the rows say they overlap, and the API and the cockpit say in words that this is influence, not proof of cause. Revisit when there are enough closed deals to fit weights and hold some out to check them.
- **Counts beside every rate.** Win rate with use is won over closed among influenced deals; without is the same over every other closed deal. Each is returned with its counts, and a comparison with fewer than 5 closed deals on a side is marked small. The comparison is also confounded in a way no window fixes: accounts that use more features are probably larger or more engaged to begin with. The fixture story is built to show this honestly: Signal search is used by nearly everyone, so there is almost nothing to compare it to, and one account looked at the quality preview and still lost its add-on deal.
- **The window and the clock are named.** 90 days is `INFLUENCE_WINDOW_DAYS`, and the same number is in the SQL; a test moves an event across each edge (inclusive at both ends) so the two cannot drift. Windows are measured to the newest event, not the wall clock, so replayed or fixture history still has a window, as `v_account_scores` does with leads.
- **Nothing is dropped silently.** An event the taxonomy does not know is stored with no feature and counted as unmapped (`$pageview` and the like arrive from PostHog all the time); an event whose company group names no known account is stored and counted as unresolved; an unusable event is listed with its position. Events are idempotent on the PostHog uuid, the first copy wins, and a batch is one transaction. The taxonomy is one table in one file, and a test checks it against the features file.
- **Expansion is one rule and changes no routing.** A customer account that used an add-on's preview at least three times in 30 days, with no deal of any stage for that add-on, is listed with its evidence for customer success. Any stage excludes the account: won means they bought it, open means sales is on it, lost means they have said no. The HubSpot task is built with the router's request builders and shown or written as a dry run only. It is not put in `crm_outbox`: the router's dry-run lead plans never are, and a pending row there would be sent by the next live boot.
- **Fictional accounts only.** Every deal and event is attached to one of twelve invented organizations on the reserved `.example` domain: the three already in `fixtures/crm-mirror.json` and nine added there, so their keys are the `crm:` keys the router already has and a lead from one of them still routes to its owner (R5). Attaching usage or deals to a real organization would assert a commercial relationship Serif has not said it has, which is why the mirror has never held one. Every row carries `synthetic: true`, the API reports how much of what it rests on is synthetic, and the cockpit shows a badge. The story is modest on purpose: small counts, some features with no visible effect, two expansion signals and the near misses beside them.
- **What changes with Serif's real stack.** Events would come from a PostHog webhook destination or a batch export posting to the same endpoint, with the company as a PostHog group; deals from HubSpot's deals API (the `closedwon` and `closedlost` stages, the deal's associated company mapped to `serif_account_key`), refreshed on a schedule. The adapter, the definition and the views do not change.


## 23. Commit lanes: a waiting lead holds its own lane, not the queue

A working day showed the cost of strict order: of fifteen leads posted together, seven were alerted 12 to 14 s after they arrived though their own work took milliseconds, because they were accepted after a lead waiting for the model. Leads commit one at a time in acceptance order only because some later decisions depend on earlier leads: the repeat-contact rule (R2), prior touches and other contacts at the account, the account claim, and round-robin. The first three are about one organization or one person.

- **A lane per lead, read at accept time** (`src/lanes.ts`): the email's domain; the full address on free mail or a disposable domain, where the domain says nothing about the organization; the company domain for a door with no person (web intent); the lead itself when there is none. Leads in one lane commit in the order they were accepted, so people at one organization keep R2, prior touches, other-contact intent and the claim in order. The same person is always in one lane.
- **One commit at a time, still** (`src/pipeline.ts`). Round-robin and dedupe cannot race.
- **A grace period, then passing** (`src/commitOrder.ts`, `nextCommit`, a pure function). The lead at the head of the acceptance order commits first when its prepared result is ready. When it is not, it gets `COMMIT_GRACE_MS` (default 250 ms); after that, the earliest-accepted ready lead that is first in its own lane commits ahead of it. A lead waiting for the model or a slow lookup now holds only its lane. A lead that passed records `timings.passed`.
- **The model wait no longer holds a lookup slot.** The prepare step's bounded slots (`PREPARE_CONCURRENCY`) now cover only adapt, normalize and resolve; the qualify step runs after the slot is released, and a model call waits in the model's own slots. Before, four leads waiting for the model filled every slot, and the leads behind them could not even start their lookups, so they could not be ready to pass.
- **Determinism.** With `COMMIT_GRACE_MS=never` the order is exactly the order of acceptance, as before. `replay:ci`, the eval scripts and the test helpers are pinned to it, so a rules-only run is reproducible. The passing behaviour is tested with an injected clock and a model the test releases (`test/lanes.test.ts`).

What stays guaranteed with passing on: within a lane, acceptance order; across the system, one commit at a time; boot recovery, the failed-lead retry, re-runs and `whenIdle` as before; second opinions still start only when no lead is waiting. What is given up: round-robin order across lanes can differ from acceptance order around a waiting lead (a later lead in another lane can take the next rep first), and two lanes that resolve to one account (two domains of one health system, or a gmail contact naming it) commit in ready order, so which of them claims the account or counts the other as prior intent can depend on which was ready first.

Measured (`docs/load-record-2026-11-09.md`): the morning's fifteen events posted together, slowest time to alert 9.5 s before and 7.3 s after, but the slowest is now the lead that waited for its own model call (D06); the seven that waited behind others were alerted in about 250 ms (the grace). In the 40-event burst, every lead other than the one that waits for the model was alerted within 253 ms, against 4.3 s in the Nov 9 speed record. Routing decisions in the paced and burst replays are identical to each other and to the CI replay.

## 26. Named referrals: captured by rules, resolved to an organization, scored, and reported apart from web referrers

Serif says inbound referral is its main source of conversion. The router's only notion of a referral was a web referrer or a UTM medium, so on the working day "the contracting VP at Wrenfield Health recommended you" and a "How did you hear about us?" answer naming Emory Healthcare were both recorded as channel `direct`.

- **Capture, rules only** (`src/referral.ts`). Two places: (1) an answer to a question such as "How did you hear about us?", or a form field for it (`referral`, `referred_by`, `how_did_you_hear`), which the door adapters carry through as `ev.context.referral` without changing the classifier's input (the prompt reads only the guide from the context); (2) phrases in the message: referred by X, recommended by X, X recommended you, heard about you from X, X told us to reach out or to talk to you, X suggested we reach out (or suggested Serif). A bare "suggested" is too common ("our CFO suggested we benchmark") to count. An answer counts when it uses a referral word or names no channel at all; "Google" or "HLTH conference" is a channel, not a referral. Words that name nobody outside the prospect's team ("we", "nobody", "anyone") are not a referrer. None of it goes to the model.
- **Resolve X to an organization** (`src/leadSignals.ts`). The words after the last "at", "from" or "of" first ("the managed care team at Emory" gives "Emory"), then a possessive, then the whole phrase. The CRM mirror's fictional customers are tried first, as the router matches a company to them; then the registry through the resolver's own name matcher (`resolveByName`), which links only a unique, strong match. "The contracting VP at Wrenfield Health" is Wrenfield Health; "Emory" alone is not a confident match, while the form answer's "Emory Healthcare" is. The first capture that resolves wins; when none does, the words are kept and the referrer is unknown. A referrer at the lead's own account is a colleague and is skipped.
- **Stored on the lead** (`leads.referral_json`, `referral_kind`, `referrer_account_id`): the words, the cue, the referrer's name and account key when resolved, whether that account is a customer, and its owner (the CRM owner, or a rep who claimed it). ## 28. Link review: probable links wait for a person, and a decision is stored on its own

A group links to a registry organization only on a unique, strong name match (decision 20), and that stays the rule. On the real file 12 groups link that way (7 of the 47 large ones). ## 35. The cockpit's state lives in the address

Using the cockpit is scanning, narrowing, opening one thing, acting and going back to where you were. A time the server wrote into a sentence (the brief's "due 2026-11-10 21:30 UTC") is converted when it is drawn.

What it does not do: it keeps no state on the server (a link to a view is only as good as the leads behind it), and a link to a lead that has aged out of the newest 1,000 opens the lead's trail but may not find its row in the list.

- **A strip that would come out the same is left alone** (`put` in `public/stream.js`): its HTML is compared with what was last drawn. Second opinions change no count, so through that whole stretch the strip is not touched.
- **A strip waits while one of its menus is in use.** When leads arrive that do change the counts and a select in the strip has focus, the strip is not rewritten. It is drawn when the person picks a value or leaves the control. The count line and the rows still update at once.
- **A person's own change always draws.** Only a draw caused by arriving leads waits.

A browser test focuses the Team menu, posts a lead, checks the menu is the same node while the count line has moved, then leaves the menu and sees the strip redrawn with the new counts.

## 39. A hosted model goes through the same gate (the default changed in #41)

The slot was always meant to be filled by evidence, and until Nov 10 only the small local model had been scored. I put Claude Sonnet 5.5 through the same sets at the same floors (`docs/hosted-model-record-2026-11-10.md`).

- **The request is the one current hosted models accept** (`hostedRequest` in `src/qualify/llm.ts`). The hosted path that was in the code had never been run and would have been refused: it sent a fixed temperature, which current models do not take, and forced a tool call, which Sonnet 5.5 refuses. It now sends no temperature and no tools, asks for a JSON output format carrying the classifier's schema, and sets how much the model thinks with effort (`ANTHROPIC_EFFORT`, default `xhigh`). The schema goes without its length and range keywords, which that format does not take; `parseClassification` still enforces them.
- **Called over HTTP, not through an SDK.** The repository has no runtime dependencies and this is one request. The cost is that the request shape is mine to keep current, which the test in `test/llm.test.ts` pins.
- **No answer means the rules decide.** A decline, an answer cut off at the token limit, an answer outside the schema, an error status and a timeout all hand the fields to the rules, as with the local model. A rate-limited or overloaded call is tried once more inside the time limit. There is no fallback to a second model: the eval has to say which model answered.
- **One time limit for both providers** (`MODEL_TIMEOUT_MS`, default 8 s). An eval of the model itself can allow longer, and its record says how many answers would have missed the default.
- **Four calls at once for a hosted model, one for the local one** (`MODEL_CONCURRENCY`). The strip across the top names the model and its effort, and the dashboard reports the calls answered and the tokens they used.
- **The eval and its gate take either provider.** The floors are the same. The manifest records the provider, the model, the effort and the time limit, and that a hosted model has no temperature to record.
- **The default did not change here.** With no key the repository runs on the local model, and at Serif a hosted model would wait for data terms. What changed is that the choice rests on a measured comparison. Decision 41 then made the hosted model the default whenever a key is set.

What the comparison found is in the record. In short: Sonnet 5.5 passes the gate and beats the local model on every field on its own; extra-high effort did no better than low on these sets; and the rules-first combine policy, built around a weak model, now costs two or three use-case answers in 24. I have not changed the policy, because I have seen this sealed set's results field by field and choosing on them would be tuning on it. That needs a fresh sealed set.

## 40. Second opinions use every model slot but one

Running the day on the hosted model showed the list's 103 second opinions taking 296 s, slower than the local model, although four calls could run at once.

- **The worker was a loop, and then several loops that gave up.** The first version asked one opinion at a time. My first fix started several strands, but a strand that found nothing due when it started returned and never came back, so one strand did all the work. It is now a pool: `kickSecondOpinions` starts due opinions until every strand is busy, and each finished opinion calls it again (`src/secondOpinion.ts`).
- **One slot is kept free.** Opinions take every model slot but one (`opinionsAtOnce`), so a lead whose route is waiting on the model never queues behind opinions nobody is waiting for. With the local model's single slot that is one at a time, as before.
- **Still never during a burst.** No opinion starts while a lead is waiting in the ordered section.

The same list then took 102 s. The test posts leads one after another, as a list arrives, and checks three run at once with four slots and one with one.

## 41. The hosted model is the default when a key is set, at low effort

Decision 39 kept the local model as the default after the hosted one had beaten it on every field. That was the wrong way round for a system people are meant to trust. The reasons I kept it (the repository has to run with no key, and real leads need data terms) are reasons to keep a fallback. They are not reasons to route on the weaker model when the better one is there.

- **The slot takes the best model it can reach** (`providerFrom` in `src/config.ts`). With `LLM_PROVIDER` unset: Claude Sonnet 5.5 when `ANTHROPIC_API_KEY` is set, else the local model, and if that is not running the rules decide alone, as before. `LLM_PROVIDER` still pins one.
- **The key is the opt-in.** Nothing reaches a hosted model without one, and a setting that is not a provider's name falls back to the local model, so a typo cannot send messages out. At Serif the key would be issued under the data terms. This repository's key has only ever read invented messages.
- **Nothing that must be repeatable calls a model.** CI, the unit tests, the browser tests and `npm run day` pin the rules or their own test settings, so a key in `.env` changes none of them and costs nothing there. `npm run dev`, `npm run replay` and `npm run eval:gate` follow the default, at about half a cent a model call.
- **Low effort is the default** (`ANTHROPIC_EFFORT`). Three runs at low and two at extra high on the same 76 messages gave the same pipeline results within one item. Low answered faster (a median of 1.4 to 2.1 s against 2.3 to 2.4 s) and cost three quarters as much. One difference goes the other way and I am keeping it in view: from run to run, low gave a different use-case label on 1 or 2 of the 76 messages, and extra high on none. On samples this small that is a hint, not a finding. If steadiness matters more than a second on real leads, extra high is one setting.
- **Each model is held to its own floors** (`HOSTED_FLOORS` and `floorsFor` in `scripts/lib/eval-gate.ts`). Sonnet cleared the local model's floors with room to spare, which means those floors would have let it slip by two or three use-case answers unnoticed. Its floors sit one below the lowest of the three low-effort runs: sealed 22, 19 and 21 of 24 and held-out 21, 20 and 23 for urgency, use case and disqualifier. The local model keeps its floors, and the rules keep theirs with no slack. `npm run eval:gate` as the README gives it passes at the new floors (`docs/eval-record-2026-11-10-default.md`).
- **The working day was run again on this default**, with nothing set but the key. The figures are in `docs/hosted-model-record-2026-11-10.md`.

Not changed: the rules still go first on use case. That is the open question from decision 39, and it still needs a fresh sealed set.

## 42. A contact HubSpot refuses does not cost the task

The next two requests then went out with the placeholder for the contact's id still in them, so the association and the task failed too. ## 43. A hosted copy sits behind one password, sends nothing to HubSpot, and runs a pinned commit

A reviewer who can click through the cockpit learns more than one who has to clone it, so one copy is reachable from outside. The server has no sign-in, its webhook doors other than Calendly take unsigned posts, and the cockpit follows the look of Serif's site. None of that may be open to the internet.

- **One gate, in the server** (`src/access.ts`, `ACCESS_PASSWORD`). With the password set, every request needs it as HTTP Basic: pages, the API, the doors and the event stream. Without it the answer is a 401 that asks for it. The comparison is on digests in constant time, and every answer tells crawlers to index nothing. Unset, the server behaves as before, so local use and the tests are unchanged.
- **Why a shared password and not single sign-on in front.** I could not test a reviewer's login myself, and a reviewer locked out is the worst outcome for a copy that exists to be looked at. A password I can check from outside before I send it. The cost is that it is shared: it says who may open the copy, not who decided what, so decisions still record the name a person types.
- **It never sends to HubSpot.** The hosted copy runs with `HUBSPOT_LIVE=0`, whatever its settings file holds. A reviewer cannot see my test portal, so live writes there would show them nothing and leave records behind.
- **It does call the hosted model**, because that is the system being shown. The bound on that is the password and a spend limit on the key, not anything in this code. A cap on model calls per day in the server would be the next thing to build if this copy stayed up.
- **A pinned commit, on its own data.** It runs from a separate checkout at one commit, so work in progress never reaches it, on a snapshot of the working day in `docs/hosted-model-record-2026-11-10.md`. `BRIEF_WINDOW_HOURS` is set to 14 days there, so the Today tab does not empty the morning after the day was loaded.
- **Not built.** Per-person accounts, a rate limit, and a reset of the data. Reviewers share one database, so one person's decision on a review item is gone for the next.
